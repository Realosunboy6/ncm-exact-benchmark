\begin{abstract}
We specialize the known technique of generating test problems from prescribed
optimality conditions to the nearest correlation matrix (NCM) problem. The
resulting instances have exactly known primal and dual solutions, and their
solution rank, near-zero multiplicity and eigenvalue separation are set
independently; when the separation is zero, that multiplicity is the
strict-complementarity defect. We pair this family with a matched-accuracy protocol:
work--precision trajectories measured against the exact solution,
accepted-only accounting, and primitive-work reporting. Together they show
that the conclusions of NCM comparisons depend on the stopping rule and the
accuracy target. Five verified solver variants were run on 270 instances with
$n=100$ and on 162 instances with $n=500$. Under all 11 conventions tested,
the cheapest method never changes; we report this as a null result for winner
reversal. The order below it is less stable. At $n=100$ both AGD-SDAJ
variants are cheaper than SBB-Dual at loose targets, whereas SBB-Dual is
cheaper from $10^{-6}$ onward, and which of the two leads is organized by the
rank of the solution. Neither pattern survives at $n=500$, where AGD-SDAJ-BH
is cheaper at every target and every rank, although its advantage narrows as
rank grows. The order below the leading method is therefore not an intrinsic
property of the methods; it depends materially on the test set and its
dimension. Under the
dimension-scaled rule $\nrm{\nabla\theta}_2\le10^{-7}n$, the achieved
accuracy varies by a factor of 383 across solvers. We also show that a known
finite-precision failure of objective-value Armijo tests recurs in a second
published method, AGD-SDAJ, where it does not affect the published results.
It is removed in the Newton method by the published Borsdorf--Higham
modified line search, and in AGD-SDAJ by our adaptation of their
near-equality safeguard.
\end{abstract}
