\section{The dimension-scaled stopping rule}\label{sec:stopping}

A common exit criterion for dual NCM methods is
$\nrm{\nabla\theta(y_k)}_2\le10^{-7}n$. It is used, for example, in the
experiments of~\cite{huynh2025accelerated}. Because the threshold grows linearly
with $n$, the forward error it guarantees depends on both the dimension and
the method. The general point that native stopping tests confound
comparisons was made by Dolan, Mor\'e and Munson~\cite{dolan2006optimality}.
This section documents a specific NCM instance of it.

\paragraph{Spread across methods at a fixed $n$.}
\Cref{tab:native} and \cref{fig:native} report the timing run under this rule
at $n=100$, where the tolerance is $10^{-5}$.

At this dimension the best and worst median achieved accuracies differ by a
factor of 383 over all 270 instances. Repeating the run at $n=500$ on a subset
of 18 instances (threshold $5\times10^{-5}$) gives a spread of only 13.8, with
medians from $1.74\times10^{-5}$ for Newton-SIN-BH to $2.41\times10^{-4}$ for
Dykstra-APM. The spread across solvers is therefore not itself a stable
quantity, and we do not claim it as one; the next paragraph compares the two
dimensions on the same instances. Newton
overshoots the tolerance because a quadratically convergent step moves far
past the threshold. First-order methods stop close to it, and Dykstra-APM's
stopping quantity (the diagonal error of its PSD iterate) is not the dual
gradient norm at all. The cost column is therefore a comparison at different
accuracies, and it favours methods that stop early. At this tolerance the
native costs also place SBB-Dual (18.5 \EVDs) slightly ahead of both AGD
variants (20 \EVDs), with Anderson-APM ahead of all three (13 \EVDs). That
comparison sits at the
crossover of \cref{fig:crossover}: the accuracies reached, near $10^{-5}$,
lie where the matched-accuracy order changes, so the native ranking agrees
with neither the loose-target nor the tight-target regime and should not be
read as evidence for either.

\paragraph{Growth with $n$.}
The rule loosens as $n$ grows. In earlier runs, outside the ranking study, a
nearly feasible $n=500$ instance exited after \emph{zero} updates with forward
error $7.8\times10^{-4}$. At $n=2000$, both dual first-order methods stopped
after 3 to 5 iterations with forward errors between $2.7\times10^{-3}$ and
$4.1\times10^{-3}$. Under this rule, a larger problem can appear easier
simply because less accuracy is being asked for.
The paired family makes the same point with the dimension controlled. Each of
the 18 instances run at $n=500$ has a counterpart at $n=100$ with the same
rank, multiplicity, separation and seed label, so the comparison can be made
instance by instance. The threshold loosens by a factor of five between the
two. The first-order methods degrade steadily: the paired median ratio of the
forward error at $n=500$ to that at $n=100$ is 10.1 for AGD-SDAJ-BH, 7.3 for
SBB-Dual, 12.6 for Dykstra-APM and 9.9 for Anderson-APM. Per instance these
ratios lie between 4 and 25 for the first three and between 3.8 and 42 for
Anderson-APM.
Newton-SIN-BH degrades by a paired median factor of $1.6\times10^{3}$, but
erratically, from 0.16 to $5.3\times10^{5}$ across the 18 instances. Its exit
lands far inside the threshold, at a median gradient norm $8\times10^{-4}$ times
the threshold at $n=100$ but $0.20$ times it at $n=500$: quadratic convergence
moves in large discrete steps, so the margin by which it overshoots is itself
erratic and collapses whenever the run exits one step earlier. The first-order
methods stop at 0.45 to 0.99 of the threshold at both dimensions and so track
it. On these 18 instances the spread across solvers therefore narrows from
1653-fold at $n=100$ to 13.8-fold at $n=500$: the rule does not degrade the
methods equally, and it conceals most of the advantage of the method that
converges fastest. A reader comparing published $n=100$ and $n=500$ results
under this rule is comparing solutions of very different quality.
The real-matrix runs of \cref{sec:real} were made at a matched tolerance
only, so they give no native-rule spread on real data.

\paragraph{Recommendation.}
Timing or iteration counts should never be reported without the achieved
forward error (or, when $\Xs$ is unknown, a certified bound on it). Where a
dimension-scaled rule is used for compatibility with earlier work, it should
be accompanied by a run to a common tight tolerance.
